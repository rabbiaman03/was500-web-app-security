Why cipher machines matter

For the vast majority of human history, maintaining the confidentiality of a message involved placing one’s trust in someone holding pen and paper and following the system flawlessly. Fatigue, shortcut-taking, and redundancy in language are precisely what cryptographers seek out, which is why cipher machines were invented to rid the system of its human frailties. A rod, a disc, or a set of rotors will follow the same rule over and over again without fail, and follow much more complicated ones that cannot be done by hand.

This collection of machines follows the concept through more than two millennia, from the leather strip of Sparta to the electronic code-breaking machine with thousands of valves. Each machine built upon the flaws in the previous machine and was ultimately outsmarted by a hacker who uncovered something about the system that its designers had overlooked. This rivalry between designers and hackers led to genuine developments in both math and engineering. The breaking of German teleprinter codes in the Second World War was the inspiration for one of the earliest programmable electronic computers. Modern cryptography methods which protect our online banking and private communications have learned the lessons from these machines: secrecy should reside in the key, not the method.
